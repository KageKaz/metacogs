I've been thinking about starting a blog for a while now. 

I guess that begs the question: why haven't I already?

I've already got [Shupedia](https://shupedia.pages.dev/) to keep rolling along, so at first glance that makes a blog seem redundant. But it's not, and I think the difference between the two is the exact reason for my hesitance to blog.

I view Shupedia as a "[digital garden](https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history)" (click the link for a more in-depth explanation). Basically, it means I can feel okay about publishing a one sentence page on a topic, then coming back a week later and adding another few sentences, and so forth. Everything there is written under the assumption that it will be continually refined. There's no obligation to get it right on the first, second, or third try. It's safe.  And because of its atomic structure (every idea has its own page) its easy to fix things without worrying about collateral damage. 

But Shupedia's design has its drawbacks too. Its technical, encyclopedia-like style makes it impersonal, boring, soulless. And while its atomic structure in theory gives the reader freedom to make their own connections, it also means I can't share my own molecules. And because Shupedia is aimed at objectivity, there is no room for my subjective experiences. 

So it's not like I don't have a sufficient motivation for blogging.

I guess what scares me about blogging is that idea of a "final product". I mean sure, in school, there also comes a day when I have to turn in a "final draft". But essays for (high) school are temporary. You turn them in, get a grade, and then everyone can forget about it. Publishing on the internet, on the other hand, is about as eternal as it gets, at least in this world. Sure you can delete it, or edit it, but no one asked you to publish it in the first place. Every sentence written in a blog comes with the unwritten presumption that this is valuable, worth your time. That you ought to care. And how can I even make that assumption when I don't even know who you are?

(As of the time of writing this, at least for the time being, a grand total of at best two people will be reading this, which is probably the reason that I have the balls to publish this.)

I'll spare you from further rambling. The reason I am writing this is because I figured if fear could prevent me from writing, then maybe I could write about fear. I'm writing because I figured if I didn't know what to write, then maybe I could write about what it was like to not know.  I'm writing because I figured that it's even better to be real than to be right.
<br>
Cognitive psychologists call this kind of reflection metacognition. While the subject matter for posts could vary, they would share the common thread of introspection. I figured my blog would be a collection of these metacognitions, metacogs. 


> [!definition]
> **metacog** (*n*)
> 
> A thought about a thought or feeling.

For a better example of metacognition, you ought to [consider the lobster](https://www.columbia.edu/~col8/lobsterarticle.pdf).

Given fears expressed above, here are two ground rules that I will endeavor to follow for the blog .
1. I will never make any meaningful change to an article after publishing. I tend to hate my old writing. However, blog posts are meant to be a snapshot of the kind of person I am at publication, not a year after publication, when I think I know better. We must hold on to that which is real. Mistakes perfectly capture my imperfections. 
2. I will never delay publication because I am afraid of making a permanent (see rule 1) mistake. 

The goal here is not to write well-reasoned philosophical treatises or beautiful poetry. The goal here is to express what it means to be someone who has thoughts. 

There's this scene in a TV show that I really like, ER. I can't be bothered to find the episode number, the details aren't important anyway. Basically, in the ER, the docs and nurses see a constant barrage of people at their worst: women who have been raped, children who have been abused, etc. Day after day, year after year, these workers have found ways of coping. Anyway, one episode, a doctor sees a particularly gruesome case and completely breaks down, loses it. Afterwards, he has a hard time going back to work. When consoling him, one of the nurses says something (albeit more eloquently) like "sometimes, we're surprised by how we react to things". It wasn't what he saw that shook him, it was how he reacted to what he saw.  
<br>
I'm writing this because maybe there's someone out there who needs to know that it's okay, even normal, to be surprised, confused, and even ashamed of how you react to things. If that doesn't resonate with you, maybe this isn't worth your time.

> We read to know we are not alone.

**Thank you for reading.**
